## Project Overview & Vision

A browser-based free-roam stunt driving game, built directly off the Madalin Stunt Cars 2 (MSC2) template: an open test-track world of loops, ramps, jumps, and long runways, driven with forgiving arcade car physics and rendered with modern PBR lighting so it reads as genuinely photoreal rather than "game-y" — matching the reference screenshot's look exactly, not a stylized or cartoon take on it.

This build serves two audiences at once:

- **A public stunt driving game** — open a browser tab, no install, no account, drive immediately.
- **A piece hosted on the school's website** — a playable project that has to survive a school's network, content filtering, and device reality (locked-down Chromebooks, no admin install rights) while still looking legitimately good to a teacher or visitor who clicks it cold.

Scope is deliberately a sandbox, not a competitive racer: no lap times, no AI traffic, no damage model for v1. The point is the same thing MSC2 nails — a satisfying car, a loop, a ramp, and a world that invites messing around. Multiplayer here means **shared local presence** (seeing classmates driving the same world at the same time), not global matchmaking or ranked play.

## Platform & Hosting Constraints

Single-player has to work with zero backend: everything — rendering, physics, car logic — runs client-side in one static bundle (HTML/JS/WASM). That bundle gets dropped onto the school site either as a direct page or an iframe embed, and it also needs to run as a standalone link anywhere else (itch.io-style hosting, GitHub Pages, etc.).

Multiplayer is the one feature that genuinely needs something off the page, because browsers can't do raw LAN discovery (no mDNS, no UDP broadcast from JS). "Same wifi" play in practice means: browsers exchange connection info through a small **signaling server**, then the actual car-position traffic flows peer-to-peer over WebRTC — it never has to touch that server again once two players are connected. Two ways to run that signaling piece, both compatible with the same game code:

1. **Self-hosted on a classroom device** (a Raspberry Pi, a teacher's laptop running a one-file Node script) — true LAN-only, works with no internet at all, but someone has to keep that device running.
2. **A tiny free-tier cloud relay** (Cloudflare Workers/Durable Objects, Fly.io, Render free tier) — simpler, nothing to babysit, and still feels exactly like "join your friend next to you" even though the handshake technically goes out to the internet first.

Device reality: assume school Chromebooks — WebGL2 is safe to target, WebGPU is not (inconsistent support). Keyboard input has to be the primary control scheme (arrow keys/WASD), since school machines rarely have gamepads; touch controls are a nice-to-have, not a requirement. No account system, no server-side saves — `localStorage` only, for trivial preferences like car color.

## Core Gameplay Features

**v1 must-haves:**

- Free-roam world with loops, ramps, jumps, runways, and at least one stunt set-piece (half-pipe or corkscrew)
- Arcade car handling: forgiving traction, plus active player-driven air control (see Physics & Vehicle Feel) for deliberate mid-air flips
- Instant reset-to-track button — no fail state, no loading screen, back in seconds
- Chase camera with speed-based FOV kick for a sense of speed
- Full keyboard + mouse control scheme, **rebindable**, tuned equally for laptop play (trackpad-friendly, no gamepad assumed) and desktop play (mouse + keyboard)
- Minimal HUD: speedometer, reset prompt
- 2–3 selectable cars (can reuse one chassis/physics rig with different paint and stats to start)
- Shared multiplayer presence: see other connected players' cars driving the same world in real time

**Explicitly out of scope for v1** (to prevent scope creep):

- Lap timers / leaderboards
- ~~AI traffic or NPCs~~ — shipped post-v1 (2026-10-23) as lightweight background traffic; see Status below
- Damage/deformation model
- Deep car customization (tuning, liveries editor)
- Competitive PvP mechanics

These can come later once the core sandbox feels right — MSC2 itself shipped without most of them.

## Visual & Art Direction

The reference screenshot's "retro hyper-realism" comes from PBR shading on simple geometry, not complex models — that's the budget-friendly part of this target look:

- **Car paint**: metallic/roughness PBR material with a clearcoat layer, so body panels pick up real specular highlights and reflect the environment like the reference image
- **Asphalt**: albedo + normal + roughness texture set showing real cracks and wear, tiled across runway/track pieces
- **Lighting**: HDRI environment map driving image-based lighting — this is what makes the car's reflections and the overall light feel "real" rather than flat; matched to that washed-out, overcast-sky look in the reference
- **Shadows**: soft shadow mapping (PCF or cascaded shadow maps) from a single directional light
- **Post-processing stack**: subtle bloom, light color grading, slight vignette — enough to feel finished, not overdone
- **Track geometry**: deliberately simple, modular pieces (the loop, ramps, barriers) — geometry stays cheap so the budget goes into materials and lighting, not polycount

## Physics & Vehicle Feel

Havok's raycast vehicle component: four wheel raycasts with spring/damper suspension, tuned arcade-style rather than simulation-accurate. Specific tuning targets, matching MSC2's forgiving feel:

- Generous tire grip — no realistic slip-angle/traction-loss modeling; sliding is a choice, not an accident
- Soft, forgiving suspension that absorbs hard landings instead of launching the car
- Active, player-driven air control — hold a modifier key (Space or Shift) and use arrow keys or mouse movement to apply angular torque while airborne, letting players deliberately flip, barrel-roll, or spin before landing. This is a direct fix for MSC2's well-known problem: in MSC2, a car's rotation locks the instant it leaves a surface, so once airborne you're a passenger with zero control until you land. GTA V's mid-air vehicle rotation (hold a button, steer with input) is the reference feel here, not MSC2's
- Speed-sensitive steering (tighter at low speed, smoothed out at high speed) to prevent snap spins
- Soft collision response on walls/barriers — glancing hits shouldn't violently flip or stick the car

Dedicated tuning time is budgeted before any art or track content gets built (see Build Phases) — getting this "feel" right first is the single highest-risk, highest-value part of the whole project.

## World & Track Design

One large open test-world (MSC2's "island" approach), built from a small library of reusable modular pieces: straight runway, ramp up, ramp down, full loop, banked turn, half-pipe. Reusing pieces keeps both art effort and draw calls down — the same ramp mesh gets instanced dozens of times across the map instead of hand-built once each.

Practical requirements:

- A boundary/kill-plane so driving off the map edge resets the car instead of free-falling forever
- Collision meshes kept simple (often simpler than the visual mesh) to keep physics cheap
- GPU instancing for every repeated track piece — this is the main lever for keeping frame rate stable on Chromebook-class GPUs
- Layout designed so stunts chain into each other (land off a ramp into a loop, etc.) — the thing that makes MSC2-style free roam actually fun to mess around in

## Multiplayer Architecture

Signaling handshake, then direct peer-to-peer: once two clients exchange connection info through the signaling server, all car-position traffic flows directly between them — the server is never in the data path, which is what keeps this cheap and fast. Authority is client-side: each client trusts the transforms it receives with no server-side validation, which is acceptable because the trust boundary here is "people in the same room," not the open internet. That tradeoff is explicit, not an oversight.

## Tech Stack Summary

| Layer | Choice | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Rendering | Babylon.js (WebGL2) | Best standalone PBR/clearcoat support, Chromebook-safe target |
| Physics | Havok (via Babylon, WASM) | Production-grade raycast vehicle physics, first-party Babylon support |
| Language | TypeScript | Type safety for a project with many moving systems (physics, networking, rendering) |
| Build tool | Vite | Fast dev iteration, simple static production bundle |
| Networking | WebRTC DataChannels + small Node/WebSocket signaling relay | Peer-to-peer car sync after a lightweight handshake; no persistent game server needed |
| Game bundle hosting | Static files (iframe-embeddable, or any static host) | Matches school site reality — no server-side app to maintain |
| Signaling relay hosting | Classroom device (self-hosted) or free-tier cloud (Cloudflare Workers/Durable Objects, Fly.io, Render) | Only piece of infrastructure the project needs at all |
| Asset format | glTF/.glb | Standard, efficient, well-supported by Babylon's loader |
| Version control | Git | Standard |

## Risks, Failure Modes & Mitigations

| Risk | Impact | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| School network blocks the signaling relay's domain/ports | Multiplayer silently fails | Build multiplayer as an optional layer on top of a fully-working single-player game; test from inside the actual school wifi early, not just at home |
| Chromebook-class GPUs can't hold frame rate | Stutter, bad first impression | Aggressive polycount/LOD budget, test on the lowest-spec real device from Phase 0 onward, not at the end |
| Havok WASM blocked by strict CSP or an old ChromeOS version | Physics fails to load entirely | Confirm WASM/SIMD support on the actual target devices early; keep Ammo.js as a documented fallback physics plugin |
| Scope creep toward full racing-sim features | Timeline blowout, nothing ships | Strict phased roadmap; the Core Gameplay Features section is the v1 feature freeze |
| Car physics ends up floaty or too sim-like, missing the MSC2 feel | Core experience falls flat even if everything else works | Dedicated car-feel tuning phase before any art or track content is built (Phase 1) |
| No persistent backend on school static hosting | Can't save progress or leaderboards | Not required for v1 — sandbox game, no accounts; `localStorage` only for trivial prefs |
| School content/privacy review concerns | Deployment blocked or delayed | No accounts, no data collection, no persisted user-generated content. Multiplayer chat (added 2026-10-23) is the one exception: free-text, multiplayer-only, client-side-sanitized (length cap + a basic profanity-word mask on both send and receive), never persisted (not even sessionStorage — cleared the moment a map is left), and a modified client could bypass its own filter. If this project goes through a formal school IT/privacy review, flag chat specifically — the "nothing to flag" framing no longer fully holds once free text exists, even without accounts or data collection. |
| No art team / asset budget | Visuals fall short of the reference look | Lean on PBR materials and lighting (cheap, high-impact) over polycount; small set of free/CC0 or self-modeled low-poly assets |
| School content filter (Lightspeed, etc.) flags or blocks the game's own link | Players can't reach the game from the school network | Filters block by domain/URL, not by association — a block on the game's link can't propagate to or block the school's own site, since they're separate domains. Prefer a direct link over an iframe embed (one URL is easy for IT to allowlist); test the live link from inside school wifi early, same as the signaling relay check |
| Babylon's CDN (`assets.babylonjs.com`), used for the environment-lighting texture added 2026-10-23, is blocked or slow on a given network | PBR car materials (clearcoat paint) lose their environment reflections and look flatter — a visual downgrade, not a crash | Soft failure by design: nothing else in the game depends on that fetch succeeding. If this becomes a real problem on school wifi, the fix is self-hosting a small `.env` file instead of hitting Babylon's CDN — not attempted yet since the CDN dependency hasn't actually caused an issue |

## Build Phases / Roadmap

Five phases, each gated before the next begins:

| Phase | Name | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Prototype | Car feel only |
| — | *gate: feels like MSC2* | |
| 1 | Vertical slice | Loop + PBR look |
| 2 | Full world | Free-roam map |
| 3 | Multiplayer | WebRTC sync |
| — | *gate: runs well on HW* | |
| 4 | Deployment | Test + polish |

Each phase is a gate, not just a step: Phase 0 is pure tech validation on a real target device before a single hour goes into art. Phase 1 exists specifically to answer "does this feel like MSC2 yet?" before any track content gets built around it. Multiplayer is deliberately phase 4 on paper — added on top of a complete single-player game rather than built in from day one — but in practice multiplayer and deployment shipped early (see Status below); that deviation worked out fine because multiplayer was built as a genuinely optional layer, per its own risk mitigation above.

### v1 status: feature-complete

- **Rebindable controls — done.** A Controls group in Settings lets the player rebind Forward/Back/Left/Right/Air control/Reset/Headlights to any key (click the button, press a key; Escape cancels). Rebinding to a key already in use swaps it with whatever the target action had, so two actions never silently share one key. Persisted in `settings.keyBindings` (`src/settings.ts`), applied live via `InputManager.setBindings()` (`src/input.ts`), with a "Reset to defaults" button. `ArrowUp/Down/Left/Right` are intentionally **not** rebindable — they stay reserved as the fixed GTA-style air-control keys (pitch/roll while airborne) no matter how the drive keys are remapped, and also still work as a secondary drive input per the original "full keyboard scheme" ask. The in-game control hint updates live to reflect the current bindings.

This was the last item on the v1 must-have checklist. Everything is now built: free-roam world, arcade handling + air control, instant reset, chase camera with FOV kick, full world (Phase 2), minimal HUD, car selection, shared multiplayer presence, and rebindable controls.

Messages travel over their own reliable/ordered WebRTC data channel (`"chat"`, separate from the unreliable/unordered `"transform"` channel — a dropped chat message matters, a dropped position frame doesn't). Sanitized in `src/chat.ts` on **both** send and receive (length-capped, control characters stripped, a short profanity word-list masked) — labeled with the sender's free-typed nickname (`settings.nickname`, same sanitization, 16-char cap, set from Settings), falling back to "Player" if they haven't set one. No collision, no gameplay effect. Ties into the existing apply()/reset() gameplay lifecycle, same as clouds/stars/snow. **Updated 2026-10-23**: originally a few low-poly primitives (cylinder fuselage, box wings) with custom nav lights; swapped for a real low-poly 737 model (`public/models/plane.glb`, from the asset dump's "low poly plane 3d model" folder) once it turned out to exist and be genuinely game-ready. - **Lightweight traffic AI — done** (`src/traffic.ts`, `TrafficController`), from Ideas.txt. ### Self-hosted HDRI — done (2026-10-24)

Replaced Babylon's CDN-hosted default studio environment (`assets.babylonjs.com/environments/environmentSpecular.env`) with a self-hosted HDRI, removing the one external-fetch dependency the design doc's own risk table had flagged as a soft-failure point on a restrictive school network (never actually observed as a problem, but a real documented risk per the Risks table above). Source: `kiara_8_sunset_4k.exr`, a genuine CC0 Poly Haven HDRI already sitting in the asset dump (flagged in the Open Questions survey as "not usable as-is — raw 4K EXR, needs an offline IBL bake tool not available in this environment"). That blocker turned out to be specific to Babylon's `.env` format, which needs prefiltered mips baked offline; Babylon's `HDRCubeTexture` class takes a plain `.hdr` (Radiance) file instead and PMREM-filters it into a prefiltered cubemap **at runtime in the browser**, needing no offline bake step at all — the real fix was picking the right Babylon texture class, not finding new tooling.

Conversion (one-off, not a committed script — the recipe, if this needs doing again): a small Python script decodes the EXR via `OpenEXR`/`Imath` (same library already proven for the PBR ground textures in the UI/audio/visual overhaul phase 6) into a numpy float array, downscales it with `cv2.resize` (`INTER_AREA`), and writes it out as Radiance `.hdr` via `cv2.imwrite` — `cv2` can write that format directly, no extra dependency beyond what phase 6 already used. Downscaled from the source 4096x2048 to **1024x512** (1.5MB) since this texture is never shown directly as a skybox — only its blurred specular/irradiance reflections show up on glossy car paint — so IBL doesn't need anywhere near source resolution. A first pass at 2048x1024 came out to 6.3MB, confirmed visually indistinguishable from the 1024x512 version for this use, so the smaller one shipped. Committed to `public/hdri/kiara-sunset.hdr`; the raw 4K EXR stays local-only in the asset dump per the project's asset policy.

`new BABYLON.HDRCubeTexture(url, scene, 256, false, true, false, true)` — 256 is the prefiltered cube face size (not the source resolution), `generateHarmonics: true` for diffuse/ambient spherical harmonics, `gammaSpace: false` since HDR data is already linear, `prefilterOnLoad: true` to build the roughness-based specular mip chain the same way the old baked `.env` did. Verified live in both day and night modes: daytime shows genuine warm sunset-toned reflections on the clearcoat paint (visibly different from, and arguably nicer than, Babylon's neutral default studio environment); night mode's darker lighting doesn't clash with the warm IBL tone — no day/night-aware HDRI swap was built (the existing day/night system only varies light intensity, not the environment texture itself, same as before this change), since that would be new scope beyond "remove the CDN dependency." A second Poly Haven HDRI (`lakeside_night_4k.exr`) sits in the asset dump, unconverted — a reasonable next step if a real day/night IBL swap is ever wanted, not attempted here. Zero console errors in either lighting mode; `dist/` grew from 48MB to 50MB.

### Pedestrians — done (2026-10-24)

Five static, non-physics low-poly figures scattered near the boundary wall at three spots (two pairs + one solo), from the `24-lowpoly-characters` asset-dump pack — the next backlog item after the asset optimization pass above. Despite the folder's name, the source `.fbx` only contains **two** rigged base meshes ("FM-lowpoly" / "M-lowpoly"), no textures/materials, no animation — converted offline to `.glb` with `FBX2glTF` (the `fbx2gltf` npm package ships a prebuilt Windows binary, so no Blender/assimp install was needed). Each figure gets its own flat muted `PBRMaterial` (a few clothing-ish tones) since the source has none, in `src/world.ts`'s background-scenery block alongside the water tower/electrical boxes.

Hit and fixed three real bugs building this, each worth knowing if this asset or another skinned/rigged pack gets touched again:
1. **Cloning a skinned mesh doesn't rebind its skeleton correctly in Babylon.** First attempt followed the sky-plane's load-once-then-`clone()` pattern; every clone rendered as a giant melted blob. Fix: skip cloning entirely for rigged meshes — each spot does its own fresh `ImportMeshAsync`. A little wasteful (79KB × 5 instead of × 1) but the file is tiny either way, and it sidesteps the bug completely.
2. **Disposing a mesh's former parent after reparenting it is too late — disposal cascades to children by default.** The code originally disposed every unwanted mesh from the import (`__root__` etc.) *before* reparenting the one mesh being kept, which was still a descendant of `__root__` at that point — the cascade silently destroyed it too, and the pedestrians just never appeared (no console error). Fixed by calling `keep.setParent(root)` (which also preserves world transform) *before* the disposal loop.
3. **`setParent()` can leave an inherited axis-correction transform on `.rotation` (Euler), not `.rotationQuaternion`.** This pack's RootNode carries a ~100x correction scale (a unit-conversion factor) *and* a -90°/180° Euler rotation (the real Z-up → Y-up fix) — `.rotationQuaternion` stayed `null` throughout. Overwriting `rotationQuaternion` outright (not accounting for the Euler value) silently threw that correction away and left every figure lying flat on the ground. Fixed by converting the existing `.rotation` to a quaternion via `Quaternion.FromEulerVector()` before composing the desired placement yaw on top. Same root-cause family as the traffic-car-pack rotation gotcha already documented below, but a different failure mode (Euler vs. quaternion, not a silently-overridden non-null quaternion) — worth checking which one applies before assuming the fix from one case covers the other.

Scale/position math is done entirely in **world space** via `getHierarchyBoundingVectors()` (the same technique `loadDecoration()` uses) rather than reasoning about the local-space bounding box under a negative/mirrored scale — a correction scale with one negative axis flips which local bound (min vs. max) maps to the world-space top/bottom, and the first attempt at this (local-space math) put every pedestrian underground before that was caught. The mirror component also flips triangle winding (negative world-matrix determinant), so each pedestrian's material sets `backFaceCulling = false` — otherwise they'd render inside-out under default culling. Verified live in-browser by teleporting the player's physics body directly (`chassis.physicsBody`, `body.disablePreStep = false` then setting `transformNode.position`) to each of the three spots, since normal driving controls don't reach a background tab's render loop reliably in this session's browser pane — all five figures render upright, correctly scaled (~1.75m), visible from the front, with zero console errors.

- **Explicitly not attempted**: playing the MSC2 reference (`crazygames.com/game/madalin-stunt-cars-2`) on the user's Opera browser and taking lighting notes from it — this session only has its own isolated browser pane, not the user's actual Opera profile/session, so it can't be driven the way the request assumes. The lighting pass already shipped (2026-10-23, HDRI/IBL + tone-mapping) was built from the design doc's own art-direction section, not a side-by-side MSC2 comparison. If the user wants that comparison specifically, it needs either a screen-share/screenshot handed to a session, or the user's own notes typed in — not something to silently reinterpret as "skip it."

### Engine audio crossfade fix — done (2026-10-24)

User-reported bug, and the exact thing the per-car sound engine's own "not verified" note (phase 5 of the overhaul above) flagged as needing real ears: during acceleration, each gear-band clip was only audible at real volume for about a second before fading into the next one, reading as disconnected snippets rather than a sustained engine note — "doesn't sound anything like how a real engine works."

Root cause, once traced through `EngineAudio.update()` in `src/audio.ts`: the per-band volume weight was a pure **triangle** — `1 - |fakeRpm - bandCenter| / bandHalfWidth` — meaning a band only ever reached full volume at the *exact instant* fake-RPM crossed its range's midpoint, fading to silence at both edges. Holding a perfectly steady speed at any point except that one exact center value meant a band played forever at a reduced, partial volume; sweeping through a band during acceleration meant its brief volume peak lasted a fraction of a second. The looping/`source.loop = true` playback itself was never the problem — bands never stopped or restarted mid-session, the *perceived* volume just collapsed almost immediately outside a narrow window.

Fix: replaced the triangle with a **trapezoid/plateau** — full volume (`weight = 1`) across the whole middle of a band's range, ramping only within a short, fixed-width zone (`BAND_FADE_WIDTH = 0.09` fake-RPM units, chosen to roughly match how far adjacent bands in `AUDIO_PROFILES` actually overlap) at each edge, where it's actually crossfading into a neighbor. Holding a steady speed anywhere in a band's plateau now sustains that clip at full volume indefinitely — directly addressing "needs to play for as long as you're on that gear" — and during acceleration each band stays prominent for a meaningfully longer stretch instead of one instantaneous peak. Verified the code compiles and runs with zero console errors through a full load/drive cycle; **actual tonal/mix quality is still something only the user's own ears can confirm** — flagging that explicitly rather than claiming "fixed" on a change this session can't literally hear.

Each clone gets a yaw rotation (varying by block, `instanceIndex % 4` quarter-turns) for visual variety and a single invisible box physics collider approximating its footprint — cheap, doesn't need to hug the jagged visual mesh, same spirit as `addBoxStatic()` elsewhere in this project.

### Real bug: the app could get stuck forever on the very first loading screen — fixed (2026-10-25)

User-reported: "the game is stuck at 55 percent." Traced to `main.ts`'s boot sequence — `setLoadingProgress(55, "Loading"); await menuScene.show();` — the literal "55" in that progress call is what the user saw frozen. This isn't the per-map gameplay loading screen (which has its own separate 5/30/70/90 sequence inside `Game.load()`); it's the very first screen the whole app shows, before the entry/main menu even appears, and `menuScene.show()` awaits a network fetch of the background turntable's preview car model with **no timeout at all**. Any hiccup on that one request — slow network, a transient CDN blip, anything — froze the *entire app* permanently, with no retry, no fallback, no way out, even though nothing about the entry screen or main menu actually needs that decorative preview car to be ready. This was a pre-existing fragility, not something introduced by anything built today — it just hadn't been hit (or hadn't been reported) before.

Could not force a real reproduction (my own test loaded fine, presumably because the relevant assets were already warm in cache from earlier testing this session) — fixed on the strength of the code itself clearly matching the exact symptom ("stuck at 55") rather than waiting for a reliable repro. Fixed with a `withTimeout()` helper in `main.ts`: races `menuScene.show()` against a 10-second timer. If the timer wins, logs a console warning and lets the boot sequence continue to the entry screen anyway — the original promise is never cancelled, just no longer blocking; the preview car (and the dark showroom mood `menuScene.show()` sets up once its model arrives) still pops in whenever that fetch actually finishes, or just never appears if it truly never resolves, which is a far better failure mode than a permanently frozen app. Verified both paths directly: forced the timeout to fire (set it to 1ms) and confirmed the entry screen still appeared and the car still loaded in shortly after: zero console errors either way.

**Known related risk, not fixed here, deliberately scoped out**: the per-map gameplay loading screen (`Game.load()`'s own 5/30/70/90 sequence, awaited by `startLoadingFlow()` in `main.ts`) has the same shape of risk — if `vehicle.attachVisual()` or another awaited step inside it hangs on a network fetch, the player would get stuck on that loading screen with no way back to the menu either (the back button only reappears after `game.load()` resolves). Not touched in this pass since the user's concrete report was specifically about the boot screen, and patching a mid-gameplay-load timeout safely means deciding what to clean up / fall back to (a half-constructed world, vehicle, physics bodies) — a real decision, not a drop-in fix, and not worth guessing at without a concrete report to anchor it. Worth hardening the same way if it's ever hit.

### Real bug: engine audio faded toward silence at top speed — fixed (2026-10-25)

User-reported: the Hellcat's engine audio "doesn't seem to be having that looping sound or holding the sound... depending on how fast you're going." Traced to the trapezoid crossfade from the previous audio fix (just above): the per-band weight ramped down to 0 at **both** edges of every band's range — including the first and last bands in the ladder, which don't have a neighboring band on their outer side to crossfade into. For the last band specifically (Hellcat's "fourth," fake-RPM 0.7–1.0), that meant: as the player's speed approached and clamped at `topSpeedKph` (fakeRpm clamps to exactly `1.0` = that band's `rangeEnd`), the ramp-to-silence logic fired right there with nothing to fade into — **the engine went quiet exactly as the car got fastest**, the opposite of realistic.

Confirmed this wasn't theoretical before touching anything: Hellcat has `enginePowerMultiplier: 1.3` in `cars.ts`, the highest of any car (others sit 0.9–1.15) — the car most able to actually reach and sustain speeds at or above its audio profile's `topSpeedKph: 230`, i.e. the one most likely to spend real playtime sitting in that broken top-of-ladder silence zone. The bug is systemic to all four profiles (same `update()` method), just most noticeable on the car most likely to hold true top speed.

(Dead end worth recording: first suspected a literal silent tail baked into `hellcat/first.wav` — checked the waveform twice with different window alignments and got contradictory readings; a third, more careful sample-level check showed the clip is loud right to its last 50ms. The "silent tail" was a measurement artifact from sloppy windowing in the first analysis script, not a real defect. Debunked before touching any code — worth remembering that quick RMS-envelope scripts can lie at exact file-length boundaries if the window size doesn't evenly divide the buffer.)

**Fix**: in `EngineAudio.update()`'s per-band loop (`src/audio.ts`), switched to an indexed loop so each band knows whether it's first/last in `this.profile.bands`, and skip the edge-ramp on the side with no neighbor — first band holds full volume immediately from `rangeStart` (the idle layer is already fading out in parallel there, so nothing sounds wrong), last band holds full volume all the way to and past `rangeEnd` (so holding top speed sustains the note). Middle bands unchanged.

**Verified two ways**: (1) a standalone Node script running the exact same weight formula confirmed the math — at `fakeRpm=1.0`, the last band now computes `weight=1` instead of `0`; (2) live in the browser, set the Hellcat's physics chassis velocity directly to a sustained 230 kph (driving there manually is unreliable to hold steady for a test) and read the actual Web Audio `GainNode.gain.value` off the live graph — confirmed the "fourth" band holds at `~0.8` (full strength scaled by the engine-volume setting) instead of decaying toward 0, with all other bands correctly silent. Zero console errors.

## Key implementation facts worth knowing (not in the original doc, learned during the build)

- **The car model's glTF material names are not semantically stable or guessable.** `Material.001` is the body paint, `Material.004` is the wheels — neither can be inferred from the raw glTF JSON's baseColor values reliably. If you need to find a specific surface's material again, raycast the live rendered mesh with `scene.pick(x, y)` and read back `pickResult.pickedMesh.material.name` — don't guess from material names or baseColor alone.
- **Paint color and car choice are two independent settings, not one.** `settings.carId` picks the performance profile (`src/cars.ts` `CARS`); `settings.bodyColorHue` (0-359) picks the color via `hueToHex()`, applied through `VehicleController.setBodyColor()` / `MenuScene.setBodyColor()` — both take a hex string, not a car id. Don't reintroduce a per-car fixed color; the slider is the only color input now.
- **Havok plugin is constructed with `useDeltaForWorldStep: false`** (`src/main.ts`) — fixed internal timestep, not the raw frame delta. Don't change this back without re-adding some other safeguard against frame-hitch physics explosions.
- **Never rotate a physics-enabled mesh via `.rotation.axis = value` in `src/world.ts` — always use `rotationQuaternion` directly (see the `setEulerRotation()` helper).** `createInstance()` inherits whatever `rotationQuaternion` the base mesh had baked in from its own `PhysicsAggregate` creation (Babylon auto-converts a mesh's Euler `.rotation` to a quaternion the first time a physics aggregate is created for it). Any instance created *after* that conversion starts with that same quaternion, and later setting `.rotation.axis` on it does nothing — `rotationQuaternion` wins once it's non-null, silently. This was live and broken in the half-pipe's right wall for a while (leaning the same direction as the left wall) before it got caught by actually reading back world-matrix transforms in the running scene, not by eyeballing a screenshot. If you add a new rotated, physics-enabled piece, use `setEulerRotation()` (or set `rotationQuaternion` directly) — never `.rotation.x/y/z`.
- **GitHub Pages deploy**: public repo required (private repos need a paid plan for Pages). `vite.config.ts` sets `base: "/StuntRush/"` for the subpath; any new asset reference in `index.html` must use `%BASE_URL%` (not a bare `/`) or it'll resolve to the wrong domain root on Pages.
- **A car pulled out of a deeper Sketchfab/FBX hierarchy can have a corrective rotation/scale baked into one of its ancestor nodes, not itself.** `src/traffic.ts`'s `fitCarRootToChassis()` learned this the hard way: reparenting a car node straight onto a fresh chassis and resetting its own local transform to identity (fine for `attachCarVisual()`'s freshly-imported roots, which don't have this problem) threw away a Z-up-to-Y-up fix living on an ancestor, and the car rendered standing on end. The fix: capture the node's *world* rotation/scale via `getWorldMatrix().decompose()` before reparenting, then reapply that as the new local transform afterward (valid here because the chassis itself has no rotation, so local = world once it's the parent). If you pull a car out of a multi-car pack file again, check this before assuming identity reset is safe.
- **Not every car shares the Nissan's material names.** `BODY_PAINT_MATERIAL_NAME = "Material.001"` in `src/carModel.ts` happens to also be correct for the Lowpoly car — confirmed independently, not assumed — but that's coincidence (likely just a common Blender-exporter default), not a rule. A future car with a different body-paint material name needs its own lookup, not an assumption that `"Material.001"` universally means "body paint." Same caution applies to the plate material (`numberplate.001`) — the Lowpoly car simply doesn't have one, which `applyPlateText()`'s existing null-check already handles gracefully.
- **Multiplayer chat and car-position sync are deliberately on separate WebRTC data channels** (`src/multiplayer.ts`): `"transform"` is unreliable/unordered (dropped frames are fine, staleness isn't), `"chat"` is reliable/ordered (a dropped chat message is a real miss). Both channels are created by `createOfferTo()` and routed in `wireChannel()` by `channel.label` — if you add a third kind of message, give it its own channel rather than multiplexing onto one of these, so a burst of one kind can't block the other.
- **The license plate material (`numberplate.001` / mesh `Object_18`) is shared between the front and rear plate geometry** — swapping its `albedoTexture` once (`applyPlateText()` in `src/carModel.ts`) updates both plates, even though only one mesh matches that material name in a `scene.meshes` filter. Don't assume a second plate mesh needs its own texture swap if you ever add one.
- **Keybindings are stored separately from the rest of Settings' flat fields**: `settings.keyBindings` is its own nested object (`src/settings.ts`), merged key-by-key on load (`{ ...DEFAULT_KEY_BINDINGS, ...parsed.keyBindings }`) rather than via the top-level shallow spread — otherwise an old save missing a newly-added action would silently lose every other binding instead of just filling in the gap. If you add a new rebindable action, add it to `KeyBindings` and `DEFAULT_KEY_BINDINGS`, not just the UI.
- **A source model's own glass/light-cover materials can be baked at a near-invisible alpha (0.25-0.4) and still look fine in the original tool's renderer, but read as "holes in the car" under ours.** Don't assume a freshly-imported car's materials are all usable as-is — `fixupLowAlphaMaterials()` in `src/carModel.ts` is called on every car import specifically to catch this (floors alpha to a 0.55 minimum rather than forcing opaque, so real tinted glass still reads as glass). If a future car still looks wrong, isolate the material by tinting it a bright, obvious color on the live in-scene mesh and screenshotting — the same verification method used for the body-paint and plate materials — rather than guessing from the glTF JSON.
